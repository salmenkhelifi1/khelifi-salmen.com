## Designing a hybrid payment system for Tunisia: Flouci and Stripe

E-commerce in an emerging market raises payment questions early. Customers use a mix of cash on delivery, local bank cards, mobile wallets and international cards. A store that supports only one of these leaves part of the market behind.

One approach is to combine a **local gateway** such as Flouci with **Stripe** for international cards. This guide walks through the decisions that approach involves.

Cash on delivery is still a common starting point in Tunisia. For example, the ChakTech storefront on this site launched with cash-on-delivery checkout, treating online gateways as a later step. That is a reasonable order: ship a working checkout first, then add gateways once you have sandbox credentials and a clear reconciliation plan.

## The Tunisian payment landscape

Local digital payment adoption is growing but fragmented across providers, bank cards and mobile wallets. Before choosing gateways, it helps to:

- Research which payment methods your actual customers use
- Check the current regulatory requirements for accepting and settling payments
- Confirm which methods each gateway currently covers, since coverage changes

Don't assume global payment norms apply everywhere. A local gateway can simplify access to local methods through one API, which is the main reason to consider one alongside Stripe.

## Integrating a local gateway such as Flouci

Most redirect-based gateways follow the same flow: your backend creates a payment, redirects the customer to the gateway's page, and receives the result twice, once through the customer's browser redirect and once through a server-to-server **webhook**. The webhook should be treated as the source of truth for payment status.

Design principles for this step:

- **Idempotency.** Webhooks can arrive more than once. Processing must not fulfil an order twice.
- **Signature verification.** Verify that webhook calls really come from the gateway.
- **Sandbox first.** Test success, failure, pending and timeout states before going live.

## Stripe for international cards

Stripe complements a local gateway with international card acceptance. It handles much of the PCI burden, offers fraud tooling, multi-currency support and a large ecosystem. Useful pieces to evaluate:

- **Stripe Checkout** for the quickest hosted setup
- **Payment Intents** for more control over the payment flow and authentication
- **Stripe Elements** for custom, PCI-compliant payment UIs
- **Stripe Billing** if the business model includes subscriptions, which many local gateways don't support natively
- **Stripe Connect** if you are building a marketplace with vendor payouts

Check whether Stripe supports your business's country of registration and settlement currency before planning around it.

## Orchestrating both: a payment layer

With two gateways, a **payment orchestration layer** keeps the rest of the application simple. A unified `PaymentService` can start payments through either gateway based on the customer's choice or business rules, while hiding gateway-specific details.

- Store the payment method type (for example `LOCAL_GATEWAY`, `STRIPE_CARD`) and the gateway's transaction ID with every order.
- Route webhooks from both gateways through one processing path that normalises events into a single internal format.
- Present local and international options clearly at checkout, with localised pricing where relevant.
- Plan reconciliation early: two systems mean two settlement reports.

**Trade-offs.** A hybrid approach adds development and maintenance cost compared with a single gateway. Whether the wider reach justifies it depends on your customers and volume, so measure before committing.

## Summary

There is no one-size-fits-all payment setup for an emerging market. A hybrid design can combine local preferences with global reach, but it is worth introducing gradually: cash on delivery first, then one online gateway, then a second, each with idempotent webhooks and a clear reconciliation process.
